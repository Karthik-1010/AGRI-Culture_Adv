# GreenHarvest Agriculture Reference Guide

## Soil health
Build soil health with regular soil tests, living roots, crop rotations, residue
retention, and organic matter where appropriate. Soil texture, drainage, pH, and
local climate affect which practices are suitable. Base nutrient decisions on a
current soil test and locally accepted recommendations rather than a generic
fertilizer rate.

## Water management
Match irrigation scheduling to crop growth stage, soil water-holding capacity,
weather, and measured field moisture. Inspect irrigation equipment for leaks and
uneven application. Drip and other efficient systems can reduce water loss when
they are correctly designed and maintained; savings depend on the farm and its
previous irrigation method.

## Crop planning
Choose crop varieties suited to local conditions, planting windows, market needs,
and available water. Rotate crops where practical to support soil structure and
interrupt some pest and disease cycles. Use locally relevant extension guidance
for planting dates and cultivar selection.

## Integrated pest management
Integrated pest management combines prevention, regular scouting, correct pest
identification, action thresholds, biological and cultural controls, and
carefully selected treatments when justified. Follow the product label and local
law for any pesticide. Do not apply a product based on an unverified image or
general online advice; seek a qualified local adviser for diagnosis and treatment
decisions.

## Sustainable nutrient management
Plan nutrients around crop needs, soil test results, timing, placement, and
application method. Protect waterways by avoiding application before heavy rain
and by following required setbacks and local regulations. Organic inputs can
improve soil organic matter, but their nutrient content and release rate vary.

## Limits of general guidance
Agronomic recommendations vary with location, crop, variety, soil, season, and
local rules. This guide is educational and is not a substitute for field
inspection, laboratory testing, product labels, or advice from a qualified
agronomist or local agricultural extension service.

## 1. INTRODUCTION TO AGRICULTURE

Agriculture is the practice of cultivating soil, growing crops, raising animals, and producing food, fiber, and other useful products.

Agriculture supports human life by providing:
- Food grains, fruits, and vegetables.
- Milk, meat, and other animal products.
- Cotton, jute, and natural fibers.
- Oils, spices, and medicinal plants.
- Employment and income for farmers.
- Raw materials for industries.

### Major branches of agriculture

1. Agronomy: Science of field crop production and soil management.
2. Horticulture: Production of fruits, vegetables, flowers, and ornamental plants.
3. Soil Science: Study of soil properties, fertility, and management.
4. Agricultural Engineering: Farm machinery, irrigation, and technology.
5. Plant Pathology: Study and management of plant diseases.
6. Entomology: Study of insects and agricultural pests.
7. Agricultural Economics: Farm management, marketing, and agricultural business.
8. Animal Husbandry: Raising and managing livestock.
9. Agricultural Meteorology: Study of weather and climate for agricultural planning.

## 2. SOIL AND SOIL SCIENCE

Soil is the upper layer of the Earth's surface in which plants grow. It provides water, nutrients, air, and physical support to plant roots.

### 2.1 Components of soil

Healthy agricultural soil generally contains:

- Mineral particles: Sand, silt, and clay.
- Organic matter: Decomposed plant and animal materials.
- Water: Supplies moisture and dissolved nutrients.
- Air: Provides oxygen for roots and soil organisms.
- Living organisms: Earthworms, bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms.

### 2.2 Types of soil

**1. Sandy Soil**

Characteristics:
- Large soil particles.
- Drains water quickly.
- Good aeration.
- Low water-holding capacity.
- Nutrients can leach easily.

Suitable crops may include:
- Groundnut.
- Watermelon.
- Carrot.
- Potato.
- Certain millets.

Management:
- Add compost and farmyard manure.
- Use mulching to conserve moisture.
- Apply fertilizers according to soil testing.
- Use frequent, controlled irrigation where necessary.

**2. Clay Soil**

Characteristics:
- Very fine particles.
- High water-holding capacity.
- Can become sticky when wet.
- May have poor drainage and aeration.

Suitable crops may include:
- Paddy.
- Sugarcane.
- Certain pulses.
- Some oilseeds, depending on drainage and climate.

Management:
- Improve drainage.
- Add suitable organic matter.
- Avoid working the soil when excessively wet.
- Prevent waterlogging.

**3. Silty Soil**

Characteristics:
- Smooth texture.
- Holds more water than sandy soil.
- Often relatively fertile.
- Can compact and erode.

Suitable crops:
- Wheat.
- Maize.
- Vegetables.
- Pulses.

Management:
- Maintain organic matter.
- Avoid excessive tillage.
- Use cover crops to reduce erosion.

**4. Loamy Soil**

Characteristics:
- Balanced combination of sand, silt, and clay.
- Good water retention.
- Good drainage.
- Good aeration.
- Suitable for many agricultural crops.

Suitable crops:
- Rice.
- Wheat.
- Maize.
- Vegetables.
- Fruits.
- Pulses.

Loamy soil is generally considered highly suitable for diversified farming.

**5. Black Soil**

Characteristics:
- Dark-colored soil.
- Often rich in clay.
- High moisture retention.
- May develop cracks during dry weather.

Suitable crops:
- Cotton.
- Sorghum.
- Soybean.
- Pulses.
- Certain oilseeds.

**6. Red Soil**

Characteristics:
- Red coloration due to iron compounds.
- Often low in organic matter.
- Fertility varies by location.
- May have limited water retention.

Suitable crops:
- Groundnut.
- Millets.
- Pulses.
- Cotton.
- Certain vegetables.

**7. Alluvial Soil**

Characteristics:
- Formed from river-deposited materials.
- Often fertile.
- Texture varies from sandy to clayey.
- Common in river plains and deltas.

Suitable crops:
- Rice.
- Wheat.
- Sugarcane.
- Maize.
- Pulses.
- Vegetables.

### 2.3 Soil pH

Soil pH indicates whether soil is acidic, neutral, or alkaline.

General classification:

- Below 5.5: Strongly to moderately acidic, depending on the value.
- 5.5–6.5: Slightly acidic.
- 6.5–7.5: Near neutral.
- 7.5–8.5: Alkaline.
- Above 8.5: Strongly alkaline conditions may occur.

Many agricultural crops perform well in slightly acidic to neutral soils, but optimum pH differs by crop.

Soil pH affects:
- Nutrient availability.
- Microbial activity.
- Root development.
- Fertilizer efficiency.
- Crop productivity.

Management:
- Test soil pH before treatment.
- Agricultural lime may be recommended for acidic soils.
- Gypsum may be recommended for certain sodic soils.
- Organic matter can improve soil structure.
- Select treatments based on laboratory recommendations.

### 2.4 Essential plant nutrients

Plants require essential nutrients for growth.

**Primary macronutrients:**

- Nitrogen (N): Supports leaf and stem growth and chlorophyll production.
- Phosphorus (P): Supports root development, energy transfer, flowering, and seed formation.
- Potassium (K): Supports water regulation, plant strength, and stress tolerance.

**Secondary macronutrients:**

- Calcium (Ca): Supports cell walls and root growth.
- Magnesium (Mg): Central component of chlorophyll.
- Sulfur (S): Required for protein formation.

**Micronutrients:**

- Iron.
- Zinc.
- Boron.
- Manganese.
- Copper.
- Molybdenum.
- Chlorine.
- Nickel.

Nutrient deficiencies and excesses can both damage crops. Fertilizer application should be based on crop requirements, soil testing, and local recommendations.

### 2.5 Soil testing

Soil testing determines:
- Soil pH.
- Electrical conductivity.
- Organic carbon.
- Available nitrogen.
- Available phosphorus.
- Available potassium.
- Secondary nutrients.
- Micronutrients where tested.

Benefits:
- Identifies nutrient deficiencies.
- Prevents unnecessary fertilizer use.
- Improves fertilizer efficiency.
- Supports better crop selection.
- Helps maintain soil health.

Farmers should collect representative soil samples following local agricultural laboratory instructions.

### 2.6 Soil conservation

Important soil conservation practices:

- Contour farming.
- Terracing.
- Mulching.
- Cover cropping.
- Crop rotation.
- Reduced tillage.
- Windbreaks.
- Maintaining vegetation cover.
- Preventing overgrazing.
- Proper drainage.

Soil conservation reduces erosion, protects fertility, and improves long-term productivity.

## 3. WEATHER AND CLIMATE IN AGRICULTURE

Weather and climate influence crop growth, planting dates, irrigation, pest activity, diseases, and harvesting.

### 3.1 Difference between weather and climate

Weather refers to short-term atmospheric conditions such as:
- Temperature.
- Rainfall.
- Humidity.
- Wind.
- Cloud cover.
- Sunshine.

Climate describes long-term patterns of weather in a region, generally assessed over several decades.

Weather forecasts help farmers make short-term decisions. Climate information helps farmers plan long-term cropping systems.

### 3.2 Important weather factors

**Temperature**

Temperature affects:
- Seed germination.
- Root development.
- Flowering.
- Pollination.
- Fruit formation.
- Crop maturity.

Very high temperatures can cause heat stress, flower drop, poor fruit setting, and increased water requirements.

Very low temperatures may cause frost injury, slow growth, or crop damage.

**Rainfall**

Rainfall supplies water to crops.

Too little rainfall can cause drought stress.

Excess rainfall can cause:
- Waterlogging.
- Root damage.
- Nutrient leaching.
- Soil erosion.
- Fungal disease.
- Crop lodging.

**Humidity**

Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air.

High humidity can encourage some fungal and bacterial diseases.

Low humidity can increase evaporation and crop water loss.

**Wind**

Wind influences:
- Evaporation.
- Pollination.
- Pesticide drift.
- Crop lodging.
- Soil erosion.

Windbreaks and shelterbelts can help protect fields.

**Sunlight**

Sunlight provides energy for photosynthesis.

Insufficient sunlight may reduce plant growth, flowering, and yield.

Excessive sunlight combined with high temperature can cause leaf scorch and heat stress.

### 3.3 Indian seasons and agriculture

India has diverse agricultural seasons.

**Kharif Season**

Generally associated with the southwest monsoon, with sowing commonly beginning around June–July depending on region and rainfall.

Major crops:
- Rice.
- Maize.
- Cotton.
- Groundnut.
- Soybean.
- Millets.
- Pigeon pea.
- Green gram.

**Rabi Season**

Generally associated with cooler and drier conditions, with sowing commonly occurring around October–December depending on region.

Major crops:
- Wheat.
- Chickpea.
- Mustard.
- Peas.
- Barley.
- Lentils.
- Certain vegetables.

**Zaid Season**

An additional short growing season, generally between Rabi harvest and Kharif planting.

Major crops:
- Watermelon.
- Muskmelon.
- Cucumber.
- Summer vegetables.
- Short-duration pulses.

Actual planting periods depend on local rainfall, irrigation, temperature, and agricultural advisories.

### 3.4 Climate types

**Tropical Climate**

- Generally warm conditions.
- Seasonal rainfall in many regions.
- Suitable for rice, banana, coconut, sugarcane, and tropical fruits where conditions are appropriate.

**Subtropical Climate**

- Hot summers and cooler winters.
- Suitable for wheat, citrus, mango, vegetables, and several other crops depending on location.

**Temperate Climate**

- Moderate temperatures and seasonal variation.
- Suitable for crops such as apples, pears, wheat, and certain vegetables.

**Arid Climate**

- Low rainfall.
- High evaporation.
- Water scarcity.
- Suitable crops may include pearl millet, certain pulses, and drought-tolerant crops.

**Semi-Arid Climate**

- Limited and variable rainfall.
- Requires careful water management.
- Suitable crops may include sorghum, pearl millet, chickpea, and groundnut.

### 3.5 Climate change and agriculture

Climate change can cause:
- Increased average temperatures.
- Irregular rainfall.
- More frequent heatwaves.
- Droughts.
- Flooding.
- Changes in pest and disease patterns.
- Reduced water availability.
- Changes in crop suitability.

Adaptation methods:
- Drought-tolerant crop varieties.
- Heat-tolerant varieties.
- Water-efficient irrigation.
- Crop diversification.
- Agroforestry.
- Soil organic matter improvement.
- Weather-based farm planning.
- Crop insurance where available.
- Adjusting sowing dates using local advisories.

### 3.6 Weather-based farming decisions

Before sowing:
- Check rainfall forecasts.
- Check soil moisture.
- Consider temperature conditions.
- Verify seed suitability.

Before irrigation:
- Check rainfall forecasts.
- Assess soil moisture.
- Consider crop growth stage.
- Avoid unnecessary irrigation.

Before pesticide application:
- Check wind speed.
- Check expected rainfall.
- Follow approved product labels.
- Avoid spraying in unsuitable weather.
- Follow local safety recommendations.

Before harvesting:
- Check expected rainfall.
- Consider grain or fruit maturity.
- Avoid harvesting during unsafe weather conditions.

A static knowledge document cannot provide live weather forecasts. The GreenHarvest AI assistant should use a trusted live weather source when users ask about current or upcoming weather.

## 4. TYPES OF FARMING

### 4.1 Subsistence Farming

Farmers primarily grow crops for household consumption.

Characteristics:
- Small-scale production.
- Family labor.
- Limited mechanization.
- Mixed crop cultivation in many systems.

### 4.2 Commercial Farming

Crops or livestock are produced mainly for sale.

Examples:
- Cotton cultivation.
- Sugarcane cultivation.
- Commercial vegetable production.
- Dairy farming.
- Poultry farming.

### 4.3 Intensive Farming

Uses relatively high inputs of labor, water, nutrients, or technology to increase production per unit area.

Examples:
- Intensive vegetable cultivation.
- High-density horticulture.
- Intensive paddy cultivation.

Management should prevent soil degradation and excessive chemical use.

### 4.4 Extensive Farming

Uses relatively large areas with lower input intensity per unit area.

Examples:
- Large-scale grain production.
- Extensive livestock grazing.

### 4.5 Organic Farming

Organic farming emphasizes ecological processes and approved organic inputs.

Practices:
- Composting.
- Green manuring.
- Crop rotation.
- Biological pest management.
- Organic nutrient sources.
- Soil biodiversity conservation.

Organic certification and input rules vary by country and certification system.

### 4.6 Natural Farming

Natural farming focuses on ecological farming processes, reduced dependence on synthetic inputs, soil biological activity, and locally adapted practices.

Results depend on location, crop, soil, climate, and management.

### 4.7 Integrated Farming

Combines two or more agricultural activities.

Examples:
- Crop production with dairy farming.
- Fish farming with rice cultivation.
- Poultry with horticulture.
- Crop farming with livestock.

Benefits:
- Diversified income.
- Better resource utilization.
- Recycling of farm by-products.
- Reduced dependence on a single crop.

### 4.8 Mixed Farming

Growing crops and raising livestock on the same farm.

Crop residues may be used as animal feed, while properly managed animal manure may be used to improve soil fertility.

### 4.9 Precision Farming

Uses technology to manage fields according to actual crop and soil requirements.

Technologies:
- GPS.
- Sensors.
- Satellite imagery.
- Drones.
- Soil moisture monitoring.
- Variable-rate fertilizer application.
- Digital farm management.

### 4.10 Hydroponics

A method of growing plants without conventional soil, using nutrient solutions and an appropriate growing system.

Suitable crops may include:
- Lettuce.
- Spinach.
- Basil.
- Other leafy greens.
- Some tomato and cucumber varieties.

Requires careful management of nutrient concentration, pH, water quality, and sanitation.

### 4.11 Aquaponics

Combines fish production and soilless plant cultivation.

Fish waste is processed by beneficial microorganisms and contributes nutrients to plant production.

Requires careful monitoring of:
- Water quality.
- Dissolved oxygen.
- Ammonia.
- Nitrite.
- pH.
- Fish health.
- Plant nutrient requirements.

### 4.12 Vertical Farming

Plants are grown in vertically arranged systems, often indoors.

Advantages:
- Efficient use of space.
- Controlled growing conditions.
- Potential for year-round production.

Limitations:
- High initial investment.
- Energy requirements.
- Need for technical management.

### 4.13 Agroforestry

Combines trees with crops and/or livestock.

Benefits:
- Soil protection.
- Shade.
- Biodiversity.
- Carbon storage.
- Additional farm products.
- Wind protection.

## 8. IRRIGATION AND WATER MANAGEMENT

Irrigation is the artificial application of water to crops.

### 8.1 Surface Irrigation

Water is distributed across the field by gravity.

Methods:
- Flood irrigation.
- Basin irrigation.
- Furrow irrigation.
- Border irrigation.

Advantages:
- Simple technology.
- Can be economical where water and land conditions are suitable.

Limitations:
- May have higher water losses.
- Can cause waterlogging.
- Requires proper land leveling and management.

### 8.2 Drip Irrigation

Water is delivered near plant roots through emitters.

Advantages:
- Efficient water application.
- Reduced evaporation from the soil surface.
- Suitable for many horticultural crops.
- Can support fertigation.

Limitations:
- Initial installation cost.
- Requires filtration and maintenance.
- Emitters can become clogged.

### 8.3 Sprinkler Irrigation

Water is sprayed over crops through sprinkler systems.

Advantages:
- Useful for many field crops.
- Suitable for certain uneven lands.
- Can provide relatively uniform application when properly designed.

Limitations:
- Wind affects distribution.
- Evaporation losses may occur.
- Equipment and energy costs.

### 8.4 Rainwater Harvesting

Collecting and storing rainwater for later agricultural use.

Methods:
- Farm ponds.
- Check dams.
- Percolation tanks.
- Rooftop harvesting.
- Field bunds.
- Recharge structures.

### 8.5 Irrigation scheduling

Irrigation should depend on:
- Crop type.
- Growth stage.
- Soil texture.
- Soil moisture.
- Weather conditions.
- Rainfall forecasts.
- Root depth.
- Water availability.

Avoid both under-irrigation and over-irrigation.

### 10.3 Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

IPM combines different approaches to reduce crop damage.

Methods include:

1. Regular field monitoring.
2. Correct pest identification.

4. Crop rotation.
5. Field sanitation.
6. Biological control.
7. Physical and mechanical control.
8. Pheromone traps where appropriate.
9. Economic threshold-based decisions.
10. Responsible use of approved pesticides when necessary.

Never recommend a pesticide solely from a vague symptom description. Correctly identify the crop, pest, disease, location, and severity first.

Follow product labels, local regulations, protective equipment requirements, and pre-harvest intervals.

## 11. CROP ROTATION AND INTERCROPPING

### Crop Rotation

Growing different crops sequentially on the same land.

Example:
- Rice followed by a pulse crop.
- Maize followed by chickpea.
- Cotton followed by a suitable cereal or pulse.

Benefits:
- Breaks some pest and disease cycles.
- Improves soil management.
- Supports nutrient cycling.
- Diversifies farm production.

### Intercropping

Growing two or more crops together in the same field.

Examples:
- Maize with pulses.
- Groundnut with suitable cereal crops.
- Coconut with compatible intercrops.

Benefits:
- Better use of land.
- Income diversification.
- Improved resource utilization.
- Reduced risk from relying on a single crop.

Crop combinations must be selected according to local climate, spacing, water, and nutrient requirements.

## 21. GENERAL FARMER GUIDANCE

When advising farmers, consider the following:

1. Identify the farmer's location.
2. Identify the crop.
3. Identify the crop growth stage.
4. Ask about soil type and soil test results.
5. Ask about irrigation availability.
6. Understand recent rainfall and weather.
7. Identify visible symptoms or pest damage.
8. Consider the crop variety.
9. Provide practical, locally appropriate recommendations.
10. Explain possible risks and limitations.

### Example: Farmer asks which crop to grow

Ask:
- Which district and state are you farming in?
- What is your soil type?
- Is irrigation available?
- Which season are you planning for?
- What is your farm area?
- Are you interested in food crops, cash crops, or horticulture?

Then suggest suitable crop options with explanations, not guaranteed profit claims.

### Example: Farmer reports yellow leaves

Ask:
- Which crop is affected?
- Are old leaves or new leaves turning yellow?
- Is the entire leaf yellow or only between veins?
- Is the soil too wet or too dry?
- Has fertilizer recently been applied?
- Are there pests on the leaves or roots?

Possible causes include nutrient deficiencies, water stress, root disease, salinity, or pest damage. Do not diagnose from leaf color alone.

### Example: Farmer reports poor crop growth

Check:
- Seed quality.
- Soil pH.
- Soil fertility.
- Soil moisture.
- Drainage.
- Root health.
- Temperature.
- Pest and disease pressure.
- Crop spacing.

Recommend investigation before suggesting treatment.

## 22. IMPORTANT SAFETY AND ACCURACY GUIDELINES

GreenHarvest AI must follow these rules:

1. Never guarantee crop yields or profits.
2. Never invent weather forecasts.
3. Never invent current market prices.
4. Never provide unsupported fertilizer quantities.

7. Distinguish general agricultural knowledge from verified local recommendations.
